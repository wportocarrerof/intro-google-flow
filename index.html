Welcome to an interactive demo of Flow, Google’s AI creative studio. Today, we’re creating characters with custom voices, using the Flow Agent for visual brainstorming, and then prompting Gemini Omni Flash with references, dialog, and multishot instruction to generate a TV show intro scene.

New to Flow?

Feel free to follow along with the demo, prompt for prompt. We’ll show each step live, and you can copy/paste the prompts below. If you’re more familiar with Flow or want to explore — create your own character, outfit, location, and title card, and combine them how you want. Used car salesman pushing hatchbacks in hell? Pilot ejecting from a damaged shuttle, the show title printed on his collar? Push it!

Ok, let’s roll. We’re going to create a 10s intro shot for a 1970s game show called “Don’t Drop The Ball.” We’ll be running through each step live, but you can copy prompts for each step below, or catch back up if you get distracted generating leisure suits.

  1. Open Flow and click “New Project”

    This will take you into a blank project where we can start creating.

  2. Create your character

    Character consistency is critical for any generative media project. We’ll start by creating a custom character:

    • Click Characters in the left navigation rail, and describe your character in the prompt box, e.g.

      a slender young man with messy red hair and a yellow sweater

      click to copy
    • For best quality output, after describing your character, click the Format button, and generate with Nano Banana Pro.
    • Click Select a voice, and choose one of the default voices. Optionally, customize the voice with performance direction, e.g.

      warm, low

      click to copy
      Save the custom voice and add it to your character.
    • Select Create Body and add details about your character’s outfit.

    Once your character is complete, click Done in the top right to return to the project grid.

  3. Style your character

    The Flow Agent is great for visualizing a wide variety of options. Let’s use the agent to create some outfit options for Tony, our host.

    • While in the project view, add your character to the prompt box:
      • Drag and drop the character card into the prompt, or
      • Click the + button in the prompt box to open the asset finder, or
      • Type @ in the prompt box, and type the character’s name to quick search.
    • Make sure the Agent button in the prompt box is active.
    • Tell the agent what you want it to create:

      Show me Tony in a variety of outfits from professional to casual. He's a 1970's game show host. 8 images, full body, white background, no props.

      click to copy
    • Favorite any outfits you like to find them quickly later.

  4. Create location and title references

    Now we’ll use the Flow Agent to create the setting and title card for our shot.

    • With the agent active, ask for the set references:

      We're creating references for a 1970's gameshow called "Don't Drop The Ball" Give me 8 options for the gameshow set ranging from simple to complex, no logos, no people on set, no TV equipment.

      click to copy
    • Then ask for the title card references:

      Give me 8 physical signs for a TV show called "Don't Drop The Ball" ranging from simple to complex, studio shot on a white background, bold 1970’s type with large round lights around the edges.

      click to copy
    • Favorite the set and title sign you like best to access in the next step.
    Tip: If your agent is busy generating, open the agent side panel and click the New session icon in the top right of the panel. You can have up to three agent sessions running at the same time.

  5. Create the intro video with Omni

    With our character and image references ready, let’s generate the shot:

    • From the project view, with the agent active, describe the shots you want generated:

      A 10s intro for a 1970's gameshow called "Don't Drop the Ball" Script: "Welcome to Don't Drop the Ball, Cincinnati's favorite gameshow!" Include a shot of the sign behind Tony with the lights flashing, a pan across the set, and a pan shot of the studio audience applauding.

      click to copy
    • Review your generations, check for alignment to the script and shot composition, and favorite the best intro — or tell your agent to run it again.

  6. Refine with Flow Tools

    Now that we’ve got our shot, let’s tweak it using custom tools created by friends of the Flow team.

    • Click Tools in the left navigation.
    • Make sure you’re on the Templates tab at the top of the page, then scroll down to Pixel Bento.
    • Open the Pixel Bento tool, click Load Media, then select your favorited intro video.
    • Toggle on any of the filters to try them out; click the filter name to adjust it. Try the ANAROGU (last in the filter list) with the KOMOREBI preset for that faded look.
    • Once you’ve made your adjustments, click Export to save the edited video to your project grid.

  7. Create a simple video comparison tool

    Now that we’ve got two versions of our intro video, let’s create a simple Flow tool that will let us compare them directly.

    • Click Tools in the left rail.
    • Click Create Tool in the banner.
    • Describe the tool you want:

      Create a tool to view two videos at the same time in sync.

      click to copy
    • Load up your intro video and edited intro video to test it out!
    • Optional Add more functionality to your tool. Try:

      Add a view that overlays the two videos and gives me a slider to reveal the bottom video

      click to copy

Tips, tricks & prompts

Generation tips

Anchor the frame
Use “camera left” or “in the bottom third of the frame” or “by the coat rack” to describe where things should be placed in a scene. This works surprisingly well.
Storyboards are still king
Even a napkin sketch can help the models understand how to frame a scene. Label the sketch to describe camera movements or define landmarks.
Avoid ambiguity
Rewriters and models will add detail where you don’t prescribe it. The assumptions made will often be generic. Detail is the key to unique outcomes.
Set boundaries
When editing, it can help to include things you don’t want to change: “Change the camera angle, keep all character actions exactly the same.”
Set focus
Tell the model what you want it to pay attention to: “Copy the art direction exactly,” “use this exact camera motion,” “compose the shot exactly like this.”
Create variations
Tell Omni to “create a 2×2 grid of four different camera angles of the same shot” — specify what shots you want to see, and feed the ones you like back to Omni to upscale.
Edit and generate are different skills
Omni is a better editing model than generation model. Veo is a better generation model than editing model. Use the model that best fits your need.

Photographic styles

Digital Realism

Clean digital sensor photography, ultra-high resolution, noise-free. Natural realism, slightly desaturated neutral color palette with muted earthy undertones. Ultra-soft omnidirectional overcast ambient lighting, 1:1 flat lighting ratio, smooth shadow roll-off, zero harsh specularity. 50mm lens, T4.0, moderate depth of field. Razor-sharp central focal plane, soft but structurally recognizable background defocus.

click to copy

Fujifilm Eterna 500

Medium format color negative film emulation, overexposed +1 stop. Low-contrast pastel sensitometry. Warm, muted pale color palette. 50mm lens, T8, exceptionally deep depth of field. Soft, diffused, low-angle directional daylight casting gentle downward shadow shapes with high-value ambient fill. Fine, organic film grain and smooth highlight roll-off.

click to copy

CineStill 800T

Low-key cinematic lighting, high-contrast mixed color temperature. Saturated deep red practical light pools, contrasted by isolated cool cyan point sources. Heavy optical halation on luminous highlights. Deep crushed black negative space. 50mm lens, T2.8, mid-plane focus, soft background circular bokeh, high ISO grain, 1/4 mist diffusion.

click to copy

Bleach Bypass

Cinematic bleach bypass film emulation. High-contrast retained silver sensitometry, desaturated palette, elevated micro-contrast. 85mm macro, T2.8, shallow depth of field. Overexposed, luminous, and heavily blurred background with soft specular bokeh. Pronounced, gritty film grain.

click to copy

Panchromatic monochrome

High-contrast panchromatic monochrome film stock. 85mm lens, T1.2. Extremely shallow depth of field with a razor-thin horizontal focal plane. Soft, diffused, wrap-around top lighting. Luminous, blooming white highlights on the focused plane contrasting against entirely crushed black regions. Completely out-of-focus background rendering large, soft, overlapping circular bokeh. Fine silver halide grain.

click to copy

Flash burnt

The single, dominant light source is a harsh, direct, slightly cool on-camera flash, aimed directly at the subject. The subject is isolated in a bright pool of light, with stark, hard-edged shadows on the ground and any surfaces behind the subject, and sharp, blown-out highlights on skin, reflective surfaces (like satin or leather), and jewelry. Unlit areas of the scene are rendered as deep, inky, crushed blacks, with very little detail. Deep depth of field, no bokeh. Shot with 15mm ultra wide-angle lens, perspective distortion at the edges of the frame, and heavy, pronounced film grain. Slow shutter speed combined with the flash freezes the subject while allowing the background or moving limbs to have a slight motion blur.
